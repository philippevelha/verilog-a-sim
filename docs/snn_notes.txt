 A first transistor version is running: the Fig. 5 neuron deck with its eight crossbar access transistors as real devices. Everything is committed locally as 2.3.0 (1e7f6db, not pushed). All 924 tests and the
  28/28 validation checks pass.

  Swapping in your models later. Every transistor goes through one technology layer (circuits/snn/transistor/tech.py), and the decks never name a process. When your SG13S models arrive, we add them as a second
  technology there and regenerate the decks; nothing else changes. Until then the stand-in is IHP's open SG13G2 PDK: its 3.3 V transistors with IHP's own PSP103 model, typical corner. As agreed, it isn't
  checked against any external simulator, and the README says so.

  Two simulator fixes were needed:
  - --model can now be given more than once, so the SNN blocks and the transistor model load together.
  - Every run that starts from rest (UIC, which the flip-flops need) used to die at t = 0 once a PSP103 sat on a driven line. That's fixed. Every run that worked before gives bit-identical output, and one old
    test was updated because it asserted the old failure.

  What the first round shows:
  - The access transistor changes the weights. At the bias it sees in the crossbar (source at 1.65 V), its on-resistance is 377 Ω at W = 10 µm and 3.7 kΩ at 1 µm. The ideal switch was 10 Ω, and the lowest RRAM
    level is 8 kΩ. Output spikes still land at steps 6, 10 and 14, but V_m now differs from the paper's equations by 131 mV instead of 12 mV. The deck's own check (0.05 V tolerance, written for ideal
    switches) now reports FAIL. That's the on-resistance effect rather than an error, and the fix is to include it in the codebook used for training.
  - Wall time is the real problem. The Fig. 5 deck went from 4.0 s to 64.3 s with just eight transistors. The number of time points barely changes (1.3×); the cost is about 200 µs per transistor evaluation. At
    that rate the MNIST network, with 17,664 access transistors, would take days per image.

  Two ways to cut the cost:
  1. Device bypass. In a spiking network most word lines are idle most of the time, so most access transistors sit at a constant bias. Reusing their last result instead of re-evaluating them is a standard
     SPICE technique, and should give large savings exactly for this circuit.
  2. A faster transistor evaluation. 200 µs per evaluation is slow, and profiling it would show how much of that we can recover.

  The other piece of work is the neuron itself, built from Fig. 2's topology. The paper gives no device sizes and draws the op-amp and flip-flop as boxes, so that sizing will be ours. It will also add roughly
  45 more transistors per neuron, which makes the cost problem bigger.

  My suggestion is to start with the optimisation (profile, then prototype device bypass on fig5_access.net), since it decides what is feasible, and then size the neuron. Do you want that order, or the neuron
  first?
